Ember

A voice assistant that lives in your house, not in someone else's.

Speech recognition, the language model, and the voice are all running on hardware in the same building as you. Nothing is sent anywhere. The screen is a hearth — and something lives in the coals.

The hearth while it listens — 240 px wide, exactly as the panel draws it. Scaled up, never redrawn.

  • ESP32-S3 · 240×320 touch
  • vosk speech-to-text
  • Qwen3.6-35B-A3B agent
  • Piper en-GB voice
  • no cloud, no account

The display

One fire, whose temperature is the state

Most voice devices give you a light ring with a colour code to memorise. Ember gives you a fire. It banks when idle, catches when it hears you, burns hot while it thinks, and throws flame-tongues while it speaks. There is nothing to learn — you already know what a fire looks like when it's about to do something.

The five states are one object at five temperatures, on a single ramp from deep ember through amber and gold to white-hot. Only one colour sits off that ramp, and it's the one that means something went wrong: the fire goes out, and the wyrm turns to ash.

rung 0 · banked

Idle

Coals, not flame. One slow breath on a six-second cycle. Three of every four display frames draw nothing at all, so resting costs almost nothing.

rung 1 · drawing breath

Listening

The only state driven by real audio. Those flame heights are the microphone level — the waveform and the fire are the same object.

rung 2 · the crucible

Thinking

Hottest colour, least motion. Concentration reads as stillness, not as a busy spinner. One bright coal orbits and wraps, like something being stirred.

rung 3 · flame-tongues

Speaking

Two travelling waves at deliberately incommensurate frequencies, so the pattern never visibly repeats. The bar above is a fuse burning through the reply.

off the ramp · guttered

Error

The fire is out and the wyrm has gone cold. Deliberately motionless — after everything else has been alive, stillness is the loudest thing on the screen.

The animation is honest. While Ember speaks, the wave's phase advances on audio frames actually clocked out to the speaker — not on a wall clock. If the audio stream stalls, the fire freezes and greys. The shape is synthetic; the clock is not, so the screen structurally cannot pretend to be talking when it isn't.

Touch

Tap it and the wyrm startles

A capacitive panel gives no click, so a tap needs an answer. Ember's answer is that the coal under your finger flares and the whole hearth quickens — the wyrm's head snaps up, then everything settles over half a second.

The flare is positional: it happens at the column you actually touched. And it costs nothing extra to draw, because the fire is already the animated part of the screen — the startle is one more term in the arithmetic that was running anyway.

Feedback comes from the thing you touched. Tapping the hearth startles the wyrm. Tapping a volume stone lights the stone and does not disturb the fire, because the fire isn't what you touched. That rule decided every acknowledgement on the device.

The voice

Chimes you can actually hear

Struck-glass synthesis: inharmonic partials at non-integer ratios, higher partials decaying faster, notes overlapping and ringing through each other. Nothing is ever spliced — hard concatenation is what made the first attempt click.

They're tuned to a warm F-pentatonic, which is the point: every pair is consonant, so the set is one family and any two can overlap without clashing. Exactly one is deliberately outside it.

Two or three together should sound like one instrument. The error tone shouldn't — it's a minor second, and it's meant to be impossible to mistake for a normal event.

One of these is never played. listening was made, approved, and then deliberately left unwired. It's 1.4 s long, and the speaker shares a single I2S bus with the microphone with no echo cancellation — so it would land in the recording, and the voice pipeline's silence detector would end your sentence before you started it. The symptom would look exactly like broken speech recognition. It stays in the repo, silent, with the reasoning written at the point of use so nobody helpfully wires it back in.

Rendered at 16 kHz, which is not a compromise: the highest partial is 6.79× the highest fundamental, so nothing aliases — and it matches the codec exactly, which keeps a resampler out of an audio path that is deliberately a straight line.

Engineering

The settings that look like mistakes

The pinout is traced to the manufacturer's schematic, not to community tables — several published tables for this board are wrong. These are the ones that cost real debugging time, and every one is commented in the config where someone would otherwise "fix" it.

What went wrong

Three events whose names lied

Replies were silent for hours. The audio path had just been rebuilt, so the codec, the amplifier, the mute logic and the network were all plausible suspects — and all innocent. It was three separate bugs of the same species, and the species is the lesson: an event whose name describes the common case and quietly lies about an edge.

bug one · the chime ate the reply

on_end fires before the audio it ends

The satellite declares one audio pipeline, so the framework routes everything through a single decoder — chimes, announcements and speech alike. A second item arriving does not mix with the first; it destroys it, deliberately: the playlist is cleared and the new file started. That is the documented behaviour of the flag, working as designed.

And "conversation finished" fires before the reply is spoken. So the device queued its own completion chime directly on top of the reply it was about to speak: 

53.288  RESPONSE_FINISHED -> IDLE        done chime queued
53.947  Reading FLAC file type            reply audio starts
54.032  Reading WAV file type             the chime lands on it
55.009  Failed to parse the file's header
55.009  FLAC decoder failed: -2
The trap
The decoder error is a second-order symptom. It looks like a corrupt stream, so it sends you to the audio format. The silence wasn't corruption at all — it was one flag doing exactly what it promises.
The tell
Short replies sometimes survived, on timing alone. So it presented as "it only works on the very short one" — vague, intermittent, unreproducible — rather than as a clean failure that would have been found in minutes.

bug two · the guard could not see

A state variable that reads idle while the device is talking

The fix for bug one needed a guard: don't start a chime while the pipeline is busy. The guard checked the conversation state — and that state is idle for the entire duration of a spoken reply, because it was set back by the very event from bug one.

The consequence
Tapping the screen while Ember answered killed the reply mid-sentence. The guard was not merely weak, it was structurally blind to the only condition it existed to detect.
The lesson
The display had already stopped trusting that variable — it derives "is speaking" from the speaker hardware, because a pushed announcement reports idle the whole time it talks. The guard should have used the same derivation. Two parts of one program, one of which had already learned the lesson.

bug three · the touch that cancelled itself

on_touch is a report, not a press

The touch controller does not announce a press once; it reports that a finger is present, repeatedly, for as long as it is there. A 400 ms debounce was added to collapse that stream into one event — and it re-armed against a stream with no end.

The consequence
Any press held for 400 ms or more started a conversation and then immediately cancelled it. A deliberate, firm press — exactly what a new user does — was the one gesture guaranteed to fail. A quick tap worked.
The fix
on_release, which was the right primitive from the start. A press is a down and an up; only one of those is an event.
Three bugs, one shape. on_end named the end of a conversation and meant something earlier. A state variable named the conversation's state and went blank halfway through. on_touch named a touch and meant a sample of an ongoing condition. None was a race, none was a hardware fault, and every one was found by disbelieving a name.

What went wrong

Two numbers nobody measured

The other species. Both of these were plausible values sitting in place of measurements, and both ran correctly and slowly, or correctly and wrongly, for a long time before anyone looked.

a default nobody set

The display ran at a quarter speed for want of one line

Full-screen redraws took 141 ms when the wire time said 31. The obvious suspect was the drawing code, and it was profiled accordingly.

The tell
The number was too round to be a rendering cost. Four-and-a-half times too slow is a clock divider, not an inefficiency.
The cause
The driver's model for this panel declares no SPI data rate, so it silently fell back to 10 MHz instead of 40. Nothing warns; it just runs slowly and correctly.

ours, and the most embarrassing

Text clipped by an average

Long replies were cut off at the right-hand edge. Line breaks were computed from an estimated average character width — a number labelled "not measured" in the source months before it caused this, and then never revisited.

The tell
It only clipped some lines. An average under-counts any line with more than its share of wide glyphs, so the failure tracked the letters rather than the length.
The fix
Not a better estimate — asking the renderer to measure the actual string. Two tuning knobs were deleted rather than retuned, because the correct number of guesses is zero.

A decision worth showing

Why the text pages instead of scrolling

Long replies were being clipped. The obvious fix is to scroll them. The obvious fix costs twenty to forty percent of the main loop, permanently, to solve a problem that happens rarely.

The screen is redrawn in horizontal bands, one per frame, and the text band is the expensive one — 18 ms to repaint. Smooth scrolling means repainting it every 50–100 ms forever. Paging means repainting it once every 3.5 seconds, and only while a reply is actually too long to fit:

Smooth scroll20–40% · permanent

Competes with the fire on every frame, forever, whether or not there's anything to scroll.

Page every 3.5 s, only when it overflows~0.6% · sometimes

A reply that fits costs exactly what it did before: nothing. On a 240 px panel it also simply reads better than a crawl.

The clipping had a separate cause worth admitting: line breaks were computed from an estimated average character width. Averages under-count wide glyphs, so real lines overran the panel and were cut off. The fix wasn't a better estimate — it was to stop guessing and ask the renderer to measure the string. Two tuning knobs got deleted rather than retuned.

Engineering

Write once, or not at all

A full 240×320 frame is about 60 ms of work on this board, and the same processor is streaming audio. Redrawing everything at a useful frame rate is not available.

So the screen is divided into bands — header, text, fire, telemetry — and at most one band is repainted per frame. The display driver already tracks the smallest rectangle that changed and sends only that, so the cost of a frame is the height of the band that moved. Frames where nothing moved send nothing at all.

The fire is drawn row by row as horizontal runs rather than column by column, which sounds like a detail and is worth about twelve times the speed: the framebuffer lives in external memory, and a tall narrow column touches a new cache line for every pixel it writes. Every pixel in the band is written exactly once — background included — so there's no fill-then-overdraw.

An invariant a test can check. Because the framebuffer persists between frames, a gap in the fire leaves stale pixels behind and an overlap silently doubles the cost the whole design exists to avoid. So a host-side harness compiles the real drawing code and asserts that every row of the band is covered exactly once, in every state. It has caught a destructive dead-zone bug, a false-feedback bug, and three of the author's own ordering mistakes.

Still open

A pop we can explain two ways

Some audio starts with a click. The cause is architectural, and it's worth writing down because the fix that works isn't the fix that sounds right.

The microphone and speaker share one bus behind one lock, and the framework tears the audio clock down and rebuilds it on every hand-off between them. Vendor firmware for this family of chips never does that — it opens the bus once, in both directions, and leaves it open. So the clock restarts constantly here, and a restart is when the click happens.

Muting the codec across the restart is the obvious answer and it isn't sufficient on its own. Interrupting the amplifier downstream of everything the codec can do is sufficient — but the amplifier has its own enable transient, and on this board the input coupling capacitors sit at the top of the range the datasheet warns about. So it may trade one click for another rather than removing it.

We have not distinguished the two mechanisms, and we say so. Both a lingering analog settle and a digital transient in the first clock cycles predict every observation we have. The project notes record which claims are measured, which are inferred, and which two earlier explanations were wrong — in opposite directions — rather than quietly settling on whichever story the last fix supported.

The board

What to buy, and what not to

One $20 board does all of it — screen, touch, microphone, speaker amplifier, RGB LED, SD slot and a battery input. It is sold under reseller brands, so the part number is the only thing that identifies it reliably.

Buy the board — Amazon LCDWIKI / QDtech ES3C28P · ~$20

⚠️ Two other boards are constantly mistaken for this one and will not fit.

The part number is ES3C28P, from LCDWIKI / QDtech. "Hosyond" is a reseller brand, not the manufacturer. The JC2432W328 and JC3248W535 (the Guition family) match the same rough description — "ESP32-S3 2.8 inch touchscreen" — with a different PCB outline, different pinout and a different touch controller.

Many published configs and printed cases for that description are for those boards instead. That confusion is part of why the only other published config for this board has a dead microphone: it inherits a 32-bit sample width that silently mismatches this codec. If a config was written for a Guition board, the pins will look plausible and nothing will work.

  • MCUESP32-S3 N16R8 — 16 MB flash, 8 MB octal PSRAMThe octal PSRAM consumes GPIO33–37; they are not available.
  • Display240×320 IPS, ILI9341V, SPI⚠️ data_rate: 40MHz is effectively mandatory — the driver's base model for this panel declares no data rate and falls back to 10 MHz, which makes full frames take 141 ms instead of ~60.
  • TouchFT6336G capacitive, I²C 0x38⚠️ GPIO18 (CTP_RST) must be left unconfigured. Driving it breaks touch initialisation; the chip has a 3 kΩ internal pull-up and wants to be left alone.
  • AudioES8311 codec, I²C 0x18 — analog-differential mic, external speaker header⚠️ i2s_din_pin: 6 / i2s_dout_pin: 8. The silkscreen names these from the codec's side, so most published tables have them inverted.
  • AmpFM8002E / SC8002B, bridge-tiedSHUTDOWN on GPIO1, active-low, ~100 ms typical wake. The one lever downstream of the codec.
  • StoragemicroSD
  • LEDWS2812 on GPIO42Driven from the same derived state as the fire, so the light and the screen cannot disagree.
