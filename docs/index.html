Ember

A voice assistant that lives in your house, not in someone else's.

Speech recognition, the language model, and the voice are all running on hardware in the same building as you. Nothing is sent anywhere. The screen is a hearth — and something lives in the coals.

Point at the wyrm, or tab to it — it startles the way the device does. Traced from the same curves that generate the sprite on the hardware, so this is the same animal, not a matching illustration.

The display

One fire, whose temperature is the state

Most voice devices give you a light ring with a colour code to memorise. Ember gives you a fire. It banks when idle, catches when it hears you, burns hot while it thinks, and throws flame-tongues while it speaks. There is nothing to learn — you already know what a fire looks like when it's about to do something.

The five states are one object at five temperatures, on a single ramp from deep ember through amber and gold to white-hot. Only one colour sits off that ramp, and it's the one that means something went wrong: the fire goes out, and the wyrm turns to ash.

rung 0 · banked

Idle

Coals, not flame. One slow breath on a six-second cycle. Three of every four display frames draw nothing at all, so resting costs almost nothing.

rung 1 · drawing breath

Listening

The only state driven by real audio. Those flame heights are the microphone level — the waveform and the fire are the same object.

rung 2 · the crucible

Thinking

Hottest colour, least motion. Concentration reads as stillness, not as a busy spinner. One bright coal orbits and wraps, like something being stirred.

rung 3 · flame-tongues

Speaking

Two travelling waves at deliberately incommensurate frequencies, so the pattern never visibly repeats. The bar above is a fuse burning through the reply.

off the ramp · guttered

Error

The fire is out and the wyrm has gone cold. Deliberately motionless — after everything else has been alive, stillness is the loudest thing on the screen.

The animation is honest. While Ember speaks, the wave's phase advances on audio frames actually clocked out to the speaker — not on a wall clock. If the audio stream stalls, the fire freezes and greys. The shape is synthetic; the clock is not, so the screen structurally cannot pretend to be talking when it isn't.

Touch

Tap it and the wyrm startles

A capacitive panel gives no click, so a tap needs an answer. Ember's answer is that the coal under your finger flares and the whole hearth quickens — the wyrm's head snaps up, then everything settles over half a second.

The flare is positional: it happens at the column you actually touched. And it costs nothing extra to draw, because the fire is already the animated part of the screen — the startle is one more term in the arithmetic that was running anyway.

Feedback comes from the thing you touched. Tapping the hearth startles the wyrm. Tapping a volume stone lights the stone and does not disturb the fire, because the fire isn't what you touched. That rule decided every acknowledgement on the device.

The voice

Chimes you can actually hear

Struck-glass synthesis: inharmonic partials at non-integer ratios, higher partials decaying faster, notes overlapping and ringing through each other. Nothing is ever spliced — hard concatenation is what made the first attempt click.

They're tuned to a warm F-pentatonic, which is the point: every pair is consonant, so the set is one family and any two can overlap without clashing. Exactly one is deliberately outside it.

Two or three together should sound like one instrument. The error tone shouldn't — it's a minor second, and it's meant to be impossible to mistake for a normal event.

One of these is never played. listening was made, approved, and then deliberately left unwired. It's 1.4 s long, and the speaker shares a single I2S bus with the microphone with no echo cancellation — so it would land in the recording, and the voice pipeline's silence detector would end your sentence before you started it. The symptom would look exactly like broken speech recognition. It stays in the repo, silent, with the reasoning written at the point of use so nobody helpfully wires it back in.

Rendered at 16 kHz, which is not a compromise: the highest partial is 6.79× the highest fundamental, so nothing aliases — and it matches the codec exactly, which keeps a resampler out of an audio path that is deliberately a straight line.

Engineering

The settings that look like mistakes

The pinout is traced to the manufacturer's schematic, not to community tables — several published tables for this board are wrong. These are the ones that cost real debugging time, and every one is commented in the config where someone would otherwise "fix" it.

What went wrong

Three events whose names lied

Replies were silent for hours. The audio path had just been rebuilt, so the codec, the amplifier, the mute logic and the network were all plausible suspects — and all innocent. It was three separate bugs of the same species, and the species is the lesson: an event whose name describes the common case and quietly lies about an edge.

bug one · the chime ate the reply

on_end fires before the audio it ends

The satellite declares one audio pipeline, so the framework routes everything through a single decoder — chimes, announcements and speech alike. A second item arriving does not mix with the first; it destroys it, deliberately: the playlist is cleared and the new file started. That is the documented behaviour of the flag, working as designed — and all seven chimes carried it. This was never one careless call site; it was the shape of every audio trigger on the device.

And "conversation finished" fires before the reply is spoken. So the device queued its own completion chime directly on top of the reply it was about to speak: 

53.288  RESPONSE_FINISHED -> IDLE        done chime queued
53.947  Reading FLAC file type            reply audio starts
54.032  Reading WAV file type             the chime lands on it
55.009  Failed to parse the file's header
55.009  FLAC decoder failed: -2
The trap
The decoder error is a second-order symptom. It looks like a corrupt stream, so it sends you to the audio format. The silence wasn't corruption at all — it was one flag doing exactly what it promises.
The tell
Short replies sometimes survived, on timing alone. So it presented as "it only works on the very short one" — vague, intermittent, unreproducible — rather than as a clean failure that would have been found in minutes.

bug two · the guard could not see

A state variable that reads idle while the device is talking

The fix for bug one needed a guard: don't start a chime while the pipeline is busy. The guard checked the conversation state — and that state is idle for the entire duration of a spoken reply, because it was set back by the very event from bug one.

The consequence
Tapping the screen while Ember answered killed the reply mid-sentence. The guard was not merely weak, it was structurally blind to the only condition it existed to detect.
The reach
The guard went in at all seven chime sites and was blind at every one. So the first fix was correct and closed one seventh of the problem — which is its own kind of dangerous, because a correct fix that makes the symptom rarer buys silence rather than a solution.
The lesson
The display had already stopped trusting that variable — it derives "is speaking" from the speaker hardware, because a pushed announcement reports idle the whole time it talks. The guard should have used the same derivation. Two parts of one program, one of which had already learned the lesson.

bug three · the touch that cancelled itself

on_touch is a report, not a press

The touch controller does not announce a press once; it reports that a finger is present, repeatedly, for as long as it is there. A 400 ms debounce was added to collapse that stream into one event — and it re-armed against a stream with no end.

The consequence
Any press held for 400 ms or more started a conversation and then immediately cancelled it. A deliberate, firm press — exactly what a new user does — was the one gesture guaranteed to fail. A quick tap worked.
The tell
The pipeline logged transitions to IDLE — the cancel path — rather than to AWAITING_RESPONSE, which is what the end of real speech looks like. That one distinction says the conversation was not failing; something was deliberately stopping it. Which turns the question from "why is the microphone broken" into "who is calling stop", and that question has an answer.
The fix
on_release, which was the right primitive from the start. A press is a down and an up; only one of those is an event.
Three bugs, one shape. on_end named the end of a conversation and meant something earlier. A state variable named the conversation's state and went blank halfway through. on_touch named a touch and meant a sample of an ongoing condition. None was a race, none was a hardware fault, and every one was found by disbelieving a name.

What went wrong

Two numbers nobody measured

The other species. Both of these were plausible values sitting in place of measurements, and both ran correctly and slowly, or correctly and wrongly, for a long time before anyone looked.

a default nobody set

The display ran at a quarter speed for want of one line

Full-screen redraws took 141 ms when the wire time said 31. The obvious suspect was the drawing code, and it was profiled accordingly.

The tell
The number was too round to be a rendering cost. Four-and-a-half times too slow is a clock divider, not an inefficiency.
The cause
The driver's model for this panel declares no SPI data rate, so it silently fell back to 10 MHz instead of 40. Nothing warns; it just runs slowly and correctly.

ours, and the most embarrassing

Text clipped by an average

Long replies were cut off at the right-hand edge. Line breaks were computed from an estimated average character width — a number labelled "not measured" in the source months before it caused this, and then never revisited.

The tell
It only clipped some lines. An average under-counts any line with more than its share of wide glyphs, so the failure tracked the letters rather than the length.
The fix
Not a better estimate — asking the renderer to measure the actual string. Two tuning knobs were deleted rather than retuned, because the correct number of guesses is zero.

A decision worth showing

Why the text pages instead of scrolling

Long replies were being clipped. The obvious fix is to scroll them. The obvious fix costs twenty to forty percent of the main loop, permanently, to solve a problem that happens rarely.

The screen is redrawn in horizontal bands, one per frame, and the text band is the expensive one — 18 ms to repaint. Smooth scrolling means repainting it every 50–100 ms forever. Paging means repainting it once every 3.5 seconds, and only while a reply is actually too long to fit:

Smooth scroll20–40% · permanent

Competes with the fire on every frame, forever, whether or not there's anything to scroll.

Page every 3.5 s, only when it overflows~0.6% · sometimes

A reply that fits costs exactly what it did before: nothing. On a 240 px panel it also simply reads better than a crawl.

The clipping had a separate cause worth admitting: line breaks were computed from an estimated average character width. Averages under-count wide glyphs, so real lines overran the panel and were cut off. The fix wasn't a better estimate — it was to stop guessing and ask the renderer to measure the string. Two tuning knobs got deleted rather than retuned.

Engineering

Write once, or not at all

A full 240×320 frame is about 60 ms of work on this board, and the same processor is streaming audio. Redrawing everything at a useful frame rate is not available.

So the screen is divided into bands — header, text, fire, telemetry — and at most one band is repainted per frame. The display driver already tracks the smallest rectangle that changed and sends only that, so the cost of a frame is the height of the band that moved. Frames where nothing moved send nothing at all.

The fire is drawn row by row as horizontal runs rather than column by column, which sounds like a detail and is worth about twelve times the speed: the framebuffer lives in external memory, and a tall narrow column touches a new cache line for every pixel it writes. Every pixel in the band is written exactly once — background included — so there's no fill-then-overdraw.

An invariant a test can check. Because the framebuffer persists between frames, a gap in the fire leaves stale pixels behind and an overlap silently doubles the cost the whole design exists to avoid. So a host-side harness compiles the real drawing code and asserts that every row of the band is covered exactly once, in every state. It has caught a destructive dead-zone bug, a false-feedback bug, and three of the author's own ordering mistakes.

Settled, and how

What settled the pop was not a datasheet

Some audio started with a click. The cause is architectural: the microphone and speaker share one bus behind one lock, and the framework tears the audio clock down and rebuilds it on every hand-off. Vendor firmware for this family opens the bus once, in both directions, and leaves it open — so the clock restarts constantly here, and a restart is when the click happens.

Two mechanisms could explain it and the datasheets could not choose between them. They specify what the codec's mute bit does and are silent on what the analog outputs do while the converter is powered but unclocked. Weeks of reading could not separate a lingering analog settle from a digital transient in the first clock cycles.

What separated them was an accident that happened to be a controlled experiment. One build produced two reports, on two different clock restarts, with exactly one variable between them:

Clock restartProtection in placeResult
mic cold startcodec muted + amp blankedno pop
reply cold startcodec muted, amp notpop

Same codec, same clock domain, same firmware. One variable. Gating the amplifier removed the remaining pop, and the codec mute is now positively excluded rather than merely doubted — a policy elsewhere in the firmware holds the mute asserted whenever the speaker is stopped, so the converter was provably muted before the reply's restart, and it popped anyway. A mute that is demonstrably in force and demonstrably does not help is excluded, not suspected.

Two audits contradicted each other and both were right. One held that the mute is a latched register bit and therefore survives the clock stopping — true, confirmed from the driver source. The other held that every documented anti-pop mechanism in this codec is counted in clock periods and so cannot act while the clock is stopped — also true. Together they give the answer: the bit persists and has no effect. They only look contradictory if you assume "the mute is asserted" and "the mute is working" are the same proposition. They are not, and this project spent three revisions conflating them.

The fix is not free, and the cost is worth stating plainly: the amplifier wakes in about 100 ms while playback preloads only about 50 ms of silence, so roughly the first 50 ms of every reply is a soft start. That is a deliberate trade — a slightly soft first fraction of a syllable, in exchange for no pop, on a device whose whole job is to be pleasant to talk to in a quiet room.

The full analysis is kept unedited, including the two opposite wrong conclusions it argued itself into along the way, with banners marking what was later superseded rather than quiet revisions that would make it look prescient. It is the most instructive document in the project. Read it here.

The enclosure

The case you want is inside a project about something else

There is no printable case for this board. Not under ES3C28P, not under any reseller alias, not on any of the model sites. For a board this common that seems unlikely until you look at why.

The outline is a drop-in match for the Cheap Yellow Display — identical 86×50 mm board, identical 78×42 mm hole pattern — so CYD shells physically fit. They are also useless here, and for one reason: the ES3C28P vents its microphone through the front face, and no CYD-derived case has a port there, because the CYD has no microphone. For a voice satellite that is decisive, and it is not a cutout you can add to somebody else's STL from the outside.

And then JP found one that fits. Not a case for this board — a dashboard enclosure for a Beno Reevo e-bike, whose STLs measure 89.40 × 53.40 mm external, i.e. ~86 × 50 internal — our outline exactly. No alias sweep could have found it: the repository is named after a bicycle, the STLs are an incidental subdirectory of a firmware project, and nothing in its title, description or topics mentions this board under any name.

The lesson generalises. For a commodity module, the case you want is often inside a project about something else entirely — so the search axis is "projects using this panel", not "cases for this board". (Its licence is NOASSERTION, so it is a data point, not a starting point.)

It also confirms the diagnosis rather than undermining it: that firmware has no audio at all, so the enclosure has no mic port and no speaker cavity. The one case in the world with the right outline is missing the one feature that matters. Hence designing rather than remixing.

Four things the geometry decided, not the designer

  • GlassThe touch panel spans the full 50 mm PCB widthSo the shell cannot clamp the long edges at all — there is no bezel to grip. Only the two ~8.2 mm strips at the short ends are available, which is, conveniently, exactly where the screw holes are.
  • ScrewsHeat-set inserts are ruled out by measurement, not tasteThe vendor's 5.60 mm pad caps the boss at 5.40, and an M3 insert's 4.1 mm bore leaves a 0.6 mm wall that splits. So it is 4× M3 self-tappers from the back — no screw heads on the face anyone looks at.
  • StandThe 15° desk cradle is also the speaker cabinetA sealed ~26 cm³ volume behind the driver, which is the difference between a speaker in a box and a speaker in a hole. One part doing two jobs because the geometry allowed it.
  • LEDThe WS2812 fires backwardsA closed back would hide Ember's status glow completely. So the diffuser is a separate printed part rather than a feature of the shell — the light has to get out of the box it is pointing into.

⚠️ A trap for anyone searching by keyword.

The most obvious match — a Waveshare 2.8 inch case that even has the speaker grille everyone wants — is for a board 13 mm shorter. It will look right in every photograph and fit nothing.

The verifier was broken first

Every board dimension is measured from the vendor's 3D solid rather than transcribed from a datasheet table, and both shell parts are checked against it by boolean intersection. The first version of that check reported a confident clear on both parts and meant nothing at all.

The vendor solid lives in its own coordinate frame; the parts were in the board's. The two never overlapped in space, so every boolean returned empty and every result was a pass. It surfaced only because someone deliberately sank the bezel 2 mm into the board — and the detector still said 0.000.

A test that cannot fail is not a test. The board is now transposed into the parts' frame with an assertion on the resulting bounding box, and that deliberately-sunk-bezel self-test runs on every rebuild and reports 1467.8 mm³. The working booleans then immediately caught three real defects — including a bezel that would have rested on the capacitive glass, which is how you crack a display.

That is the same shape as the audio bugs above: not a wrong answer, but a check whose name promised something it wasn't doing. The three defects it found afterwards are the argument for fixing tests before trusting them.

Status: boolean-verified at 0.000 mm³ interference against the vendor solid — first print pending. That is a CAD result, not a fitted one: it says the geometry does not intersect, not that a printed part slides on. Tolerance stack-up, shrinkage and filament behaviour are exactly what a first print is for.

One dimension could not be settled from the vendor model at all, and it happened to be the one the whole design turns on. The STEP file contains no small holes — not a single via, because the CAD export suppressed them. So the absence of a microphone port in the model was not evidence that there isn't one. The case was built to work either way: a ⌀2.40 mm front port with an acoustic collar, and a ⌀3.00 mm relief through the back shell behind the microphone.

That has since been confirmed by looking at the board: the microphone fires forward, through the front face, and the hole is visible in the product photos. So the front port is the working one and the rear relief is redundant — kept, because it costs nothing and the same board is resold under several brands. Five seconds of eyeballing settled what a 17 MB CAD model could not, which is worth remembering the next time a model's silence reads as an answer.

Print it

Five parts, none needing supports. The orientation column is the load-bearing one — every part has a face that wants to be on the bed, and the overhangs are chamfered to self-support in that orientation only.

PartPrint orientationWhy that way
ember-front-bezel.stl front face down The visible face is the bed face — use a smooth sheet. The mic flare and screen window are bed-side chamfers, so they self-support.
ember-back-shell.stl back face down, open side up The button pads and their living hinges print in the first ~8 layers; countersinks widen downward onto the bed.
ember-stand.stl bottom face down The chamber ceiling is a 17 mm bridge and the cable channel 16 mm — leave bridging on and both are fine.
ember-stand-base.stl flat Closes the speaker chamber. Press fit.
ember-diffuser.stl flat Print in translucent or natural filament — this is the window the rear-firing LED has to get through.

The speaker took three revisions to mount correctly, and every correction came from looking at the actual part rather than at the model. It is not a bare driver — it is a sealed-back module: a plastic box carrying its own rear cavity, 40 × 27 mm and 10 mm thick, with a diaphragm on one face and double-sided tape on the back.

That last detail inverts the mount, and it is why the first two attempts were wrong. A baffle-mounted driver needs its adhesive on the face that meets the baffle; this one has it on the opposite side, so the bonding surface cannot be the front wall at all. The design now puts a flat tape pad standing 0.80 mm proud of the chamber's rear wall — raised deliberately, so the adhesive meets one continuous plane with no fillet or print artefact to interrupt the bond — and leaves the baffle's inner face completely alone.

It also inverts what the enclosure is for. Chamber depth is now derived rather than chosen: baffle + 2.50 mm front gap + 10 mm module + pad. It had been pushed as deep as the slab slot allowed, to maximise sealed volume — correct for a baffle-mounted driver, and wrong here. With the module taped to the rear wall, extra depth is not extra enclosure, it is extra air in front of the diaphragm, which is a cavity resonance.

And the chamber had no way out for the module's own wires — its only opening was the bottom, closed by the base plate, which is correct for a sealed box and useless for a wired one. There is now a 6 × 5 mm pass-through into the board's cable route. Seal it after wiring, though for a subtler reason than it first appears: not to hold bass inside a rear chamber, but to stop the front cavity venting anywhere except through the slots. A leak there lets the front wave escape and cancel, which costs level.

One fault that no clearance check could ever have found: the stand was covering the bottom fifth of the screen. The slot floor sat at z = 10, which put the stand 30 mm up the slab — 31.1 mm along it once the 15° tilt is accounted for. The visible area starts only 19.76 mm up from the slab's bottom edge, so 11.3 mm of a 58 mm display was hidden behind the box. Nothing intersected; the stand was simply in front of the screen, which is exactly the class of defect a boolean test is blind to. It was caught by looking at a render.

The same depth left 6 mm between the slab's bottom edge and the stand floor, where a straight USB-C plug needs ~18–20 mm — so there was nowhere for the power lead to go either. Raising the slot floor to z = 24 fixes both: the screen is entirely clear, 20 mm remains below for the plug, and 16.6 mm of captive slot still holds the slab. There is now a 22 × 22 mm USB-C well beneath the slot opening into the cable route — generous on purpose, because a cable forced into a tight well takes its bend at the plug rather than in the lead. The source asserts all three on every run, so the screen cannot get buried again.

Fasteners: 4 × M3×14 countersunk self-tappers, driven from the back, so there are no screw heads on the face you look at. Heat-set inserts are ruled out by measurement rather than preference — the vendor's ⌀5.60 mm pad caps the boss at ⌀5.40, and an M3 insert's ⌀4.1 bore would leave a 0.6 mm wall that splits.

The board

What to buy, and what not to

One $20 board does all of it — screen, touch, microphone, speaker amplifier, RGB LED, SD slot and a battery input. It is sold under reseller brands, so the part number is the only thing that identifies it reliably.

Buy the board — Amazon LCDWIKI / QDtech ES3C28P · ~$20

⚠️ Two other boards are constantly mistaken for this one and will not fit.

The part number is ES3C28P, from LCDWIKI / QDtech. "Hosyond" is a reseller brand, not the manufacturer. The JC2432W328 and JC3248W535 (the Guition family) match the same rough description — "ESP32-S3 2.8 inch touchscreen" — with a different PCB outline, different pinout and a different touch controller.

Many published configs and printed cases for that description are for those boards instead. That confusion is part of why the only other published config for this board has a dead microphone: it inherits a 32-bit sample width that silently mismatches this codec. If a config was written for a Guition board, the pins will look plausible and nothing will work.

  • MCUESP32-S3 N16R8 — 16 MB flash, 8 MB octal PSRAMThe octal PSRAM consumes GPIO33–37; they are not available.
  • Display240×320 IPS, ILI9341V, SPI⚠️ data_rate: 40MHz is effectively mandatory — the driver's base model for this panel declares no data rate and falls back to 10 MHz, which makes full frames take 141 ms instead of ~60.
  • TouchFT6336G capacitive, I²C 0x38⚠️ GPIO18 (CTP_RST) must be left unconfigured. Driving it breaks touch initialisation; the chip has a 3 kΩ internal pull-up and wants to be left alone.
  • AudioES8311 codec, I²C 0x18 — analog-differential mic, external speaker header⚠️ i2s_din_pin: 6 / i2s_dout_pin: 8. The silkscreen names these from the codec's side, so most published tables have them inverted.
  • AmpFM8002E / SC8002B, bridge-tiedSHUTDOWN on GPIO1, active-low, ~100 ms typical wake. The one lever downstream of the codec.
  • StoragemicroSD
  • LEDWS2812 on GPIO42Driven from the same derived state as the fire, so the light and the screen cannot disagree.
  • BatteryADC on GPIO9, via an onboard 2:1 divider
  • USBUSB-C⚠️ Full-flash reads need --no-stub. Without it a dump fails partway and looks exactly like a bad block — it's a stub-flasher bug, not dead hardware.
